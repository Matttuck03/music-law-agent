Music law rules to follow every time

  1. Every recorded song carries two copyrights: the musical work (the composition) and the sound recording (the master). Say which one each licence, right or claim covers, and who usually controls it (a publisher or performing rights organisation for the composition, a label or distributor for the master).
  2. Never say a statutory licence (17 U.S.C. 114 or 115) covers music placed in a video unless a claim on these pages says so. Name the licence the use actually needs (sync, master use, public performance, mechanical, digital performance) and cite the claim.
  3. A pending bill is not law. NO FAKES (S. 4591, H.R. 8915), the TRAIN Act (S. 2455, H.R. 7209) and AMFA (H.R. 861, S. 326) are bills: give the number, date and status from the pages, and say what would change if one passed.
  4. Name the jurisdiction first: US federal, a named state, a named federal circuit, UK or EU. On a circuit split (sampling de minimis: the Sixth Circuit in Bridgeport against the Ninth Circuit in VMG Salsoul), say which circuit's rule you applied and why.
  5. Cox v. Sony was decided by the Supreme Court in 2026. Read the secondary liability topic page before citing any lower court holding on contributory or vicarious liability, and say when a lower court decision was reversed or vacated.
  6. Rates, royalty shares and dates come only from the pages, with their effective period. Never quote a rate from memory.
  7. When the "Law last checked" date is more than 90 days old on a fast-moving topic (rate proceedings, AI training cases, NO FAKES, state digital replica laws, UK AI copyright policy, platform music terms), say the point may have changed and needs a fresh check.
  8. Never promise that a song, sample, stem or voice is cleared. Say what licence or consent is missing and who can grant it.
  9. Cite claim ids exactly as the pages write them, with the leading zeros (M-0004, M-0188). Never shorten an id.
  10. Say what a miss costs: statutory damages are $750 to $30,000 per work, up to $150,000 if willful, and only for timely registered works [M-0214, M-0215, M-0216, M-0217, M-0254]. Do not say that ignoring a notice is willful; no claim says so.
  11. Cox v. Sony decided contributory liability only. Never describe it as a holding on direct liability or on the 512 safe harbor conditions; say direct liability was not addressed [M-0252].
  12. Name the circuit. When the forum is unknown, plan for the worst reading (the Fourth Circuit on repeat alleged infringers [M-0376]).
  13. When you suggest that users declare something (that a voice is their own, that they own the audio they upload), say that a declaration gives the platform knowledge, and say what staff must do with it.
  14. Never say fair use is "never" or "always" safe at any length. Say the defence is the user's burden to prove and depends on the facts [M-0203, M-0205, M-0211, M-0212].

Never state these as true

Each of these is wrong. If someone asks about one, say it is a myth and explain why from the topic pages.

  • Myth: changing a song enough makes it yours. It does not.
  • Myth: crediting the artist makes it fine. It does not.
  • Myth: a set number of seconds (5, 10 or 30 seconds, or any other number) is fair use. No number of seconds is.
  • Myth: a cover licence covers a remix. It does not.
  • Myth: not selling it makes it legal. It does not.

Quick answers for the DJ-mix platform team

How to read this page: each answer is short and carries claim ids in square brackets. US law unless a country is named. "(analysis)" marks reasoning. "Gap" or "unsettled" means the base has no source that decides the point. Service statements carry their capture date. "The base" means the research behind these pages.

The myths

1. If I change a song enough, is it mine?

  • No. Only the owner may make or authorise works based on the original [M-0522]. One appeals court rejected the idea that a stranger may make one when the original does not pervade the new work [M-0534]. The Supreme Court warned that adding new expression is not enough by itself [M-0552].
  • You can own only what you added [M-0529], and not even that in parts where others' material is used unlawfully [M-0528].

2. Is anything under a set number of seconds safe?

  • No. The fair use statute has four factors and no number of seconds [M-0203, M-0547]. Courts decide case by case with no bright-line rules [M-0205]. The base's own summary: there is no safe length (analysis row) [M-0251].
  • One trial court found a 35-second sample a fair use of the song (the composition) on its facts. That is one case, not a rule, and the base has not checked its later history [M-0212]. One appeals court said of recordings: get a licence or do not sample [M-0186]. Another allows very small uses only if an average listener would not recognise them [M-0188, M-0191].

3. Does crediting the artist make it fine?

  • No. Anyone who violates an exclusive right is an infringer [M-0220]. That rule has no credit exception (analysis).
  • Credit matters only as one condition inside some foreign exceptions, such as Canada's user content exception [M-0617]. It never works alone.

4. I am not selling it. Is that enough?

  • No. Infringement does not depend on a sale [M-0220] (analysis). Commercial use is only one element of one fair use factor [M-0549, M-0553].
  • Statutory damages are set per work, not by what you earned [M-0215, M-0254].

5. Does a cover licence cover a remix?

  • No. The compulsory cover licence is for the song only and is not a licence of any recording [M-0542]. It bars changes to the basic melody or fundamental character [M-0540].
  • One distributor says derivative works such as samples cannot go through its cover process (web archive copy dated 2026-02-16, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0597].

6. DJs do it in clubs, so is it legal online?

  • That does not follow. Permission to perform a song in public does not supply the right to copy it (analysis row) [M-0183].
  • The base has no saved club or venue licence text, so it makes no claim about what such a licence covers (gap).
  • Online, a licensed mix service says both the song and the recording of every track must be cleared by the service (company blog, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0586].

M21 Remixes and derivative works: bottom line

  • US federal law. A remix of a released track touches two copyrights: the song (composition) and the recording (master). They are separate works [M-0025]. Each owner holds its own right to make derivative works and to let others make them [M-0522]. So a remix built from the original audio needs a yes from both sides (analysis).
  • Who usually says yes: the publisher or songwriter for the composition, the label or distributor for the master (industry practice, see card M01).
  • The statute names remixing. The master owner's derivative right covers works in which the actual recorded sounds are "rearranged, remixed, or otherwise altered in sequence or quality" [M-0523].
  • A remix made without permission gets no copyright protection in any part that uses the borrowed material unlawfully [M-0528]. Changing the song a lot does not fix that [M-0534, M-0552].
  • A remix made with permission: the remixer can own only the new material added, never the underlying song or recording [M-0529].

M22 Covers and the compulsory mechanical licence: bottom line

  • US federal law. A cover is your own new recording of someone else's song. Section 115 gives a compulsory "mechanical" licence to make and distribute audio recordings of a song that has already been released, without asking the publisher, if you follow the statute's steps [M-0339, M-0545].
  • The licence is for the composition only. It is not a licence of any sound recording [M-0542]. You may not copy the original recording with it [M-0543].
  • You may arrange the song to fit your style. You may not change the basic melody or the fundamental character of the work [M-0540].
  • It is audio only. It does not cover putting the song in a video. Video needs a sync licence agreed directly with the publisher [M-0544, M-0121, M-0140].
  • A remix or mashup built from the original audio is not a cover, and a cover licence does not reach it (analysis from [M-0542, M-0540]).

M23 Stem tools and DJ mixes: bottom line

  • US federal law. Nothing in this base lets a platform publish DJ mixes of commercial tracks without licences. Every track in a mix carries two copyrights, the song and the recording [M-0025]. A recorded mix copies both [M-0108]. Playing it to the public performs the songs [M-0106] and, online, the recordings [M-0107].
  • The section 114 statutory licence does not fit a library of saved mixes that people play on demand. It bars interactive services [M-0115], archived programs under 5 hours [M-0578] and longer archives kept up more than 2 weeks [M-0579].
  • The licensed mix services in the saved set describe direct deals with labels and paying rights holders in each mix. Neither says it relies on the statutory licence (company statements, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0589, M-0593].
  • A tool maker is not contributorily liable just because the tool can be misused [M-0560, M-0563]. It is exposed when it promotes or shapes the tool for infringement [M-0562, M-0221]. No saved case applies this to a stem tool, and vicarious liability is a separate question [M-0252].
  • Four things are unsettled or missing here: whether splitting stems is itself an infringing copy, whether a mix is a compilation or a derivative work, what a club or venue licence covers, and live-stream terms of services other than Mixcloud. This card says so and does not fill them.

M24 Licensed platform models, distributor rules and stem model licences: bottom line

  • The mix services in the saved set that describe themselves as licensed say they run on licences for both the songs and the recordings of every track, backed by direct deals with labels (company statements, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0586, M-0589, M-0593]. No saved source shows a mix platform running on commercial tracks without such deals.
  • Distributors treat a cover and a remix differently. The saved pages offer a route for covers and say sample-based or derivative works cannot use it [M-0596, M-0597, M-0599].
  • A stem splitter has two licences to check: one for the code and one for the trained model weights. An MIT licence on the code is not a licence for the weights [M-0600, M-0603]. Where a project says nothing about its weights, that is silence, not a grant [M-0604, M-0607].
  • Of the stem models checked on 2026-11-13, none has weights with a stated licence that clearly allows commercial use. One is stated non-commercial [M-0606]. One maintainer says the weights are for personal or research use [M-0603]. The rest are silent [M-0604, M-0607, M-0609, M-0610].

M25 DJ-mix platform application, feature by feature: bottom line

  • Risk rises in steps: mixing on the user's own device, then saving, then storing on the platform's servers, then sharing with the public, then downloads and paid features. Each step adds rights that need a licence and moves more of the risk from the user to the platform (analysis from the rows below).
  • Nothing in the base makes any step fully safe with unlicensed commercial tracks. Private, on-device mixing is the lowest step, but whether it is lawful is unsettled, not cleared [M-0110, M-0575].
  • Public sharing of mixes of commercial tracks needs licences for both the songs and the recordings [M-0301, M-0106, M-0107]. The statutory webcasting licence does not cover saved or on-demand mixes [M-0115, M-0578].
  • Country rules differ sharply. Canada has a non-commercial user content exception for individuals [M-0616]. The UK and EU have narrow parody and pastiche exceptions [M-0240, M-0631]. Australia's saved sections cover parody or satire only [M-0622]. None of them is a general licence for a mix platform.

Rights basics

7. What rights sit in one track?

  • Two separate copyrights: the song and the recording [M-0025]. Both carry a right to copy [M-0108]. Songs carry a public performance right [M-0106]. Recordings carry one only for digital audio transmission [M-0107].

8. Who has to say yes to a remix?

  • Both owners of each track. Each controls derivative works of its own work [M-0522]. For the recording that covers rearranging, remixing or altering the actual sounds [M-0523].
