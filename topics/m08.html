M08 DMCA 512 safe harbor for a platform that hosts user mixes

Part 1 of 2: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: medium.

Answers questions like: Does the safe harbor protect a platform when users upload mixes of commercial songs; What must a platform do to qualify for 512(c); Does a platform need a designated agent and when does it expire; What is a repeat infringer policy and what must it say; What happens after a counter notice; Does processing or re-encoding user audio change the answer

Parts: 1 2

Bottom line

US federal law: 17 U.S.C. 512(c) can shield a platform from copyright damages for user uploaded videos that carry someone else's song, but only if the platform meets every condition [M-0343, M-0344, M-0345, M-0346, M-0347, M-0348, M-0357]. The safe harbor can apply to claims on both the composition and the master copyright (analysis), because it limits liability for the infringement claim itself, not a licence. It is not a licence and not a defence for the platform's own conduct. Section 230 does not add anything for copyright [M-0401, M-0402].

  • Core conditions: no actual or red flag knowledge, prompt removal, no direct financial benefit with the right and ability to control, a designated agent, and a real repeat infringer policy [M-0344, M-0345, M-0346, M-0347, M-0349, M-0357].

Rules

Statute (17 U.S.C. 512)

  • 512(c) covers infringement by reason of storage at the direction of a user on the provider's system [M-0343].
  • Knowledge: the provider must lack actual knowledge of infringement [M-0344]. Without actual knowledge it must not be aware of facts or circumstances making infringement apparent, called red flag knowledge [M-0345].
  • On gaining knowledge or awareness the provider must act expeditiously to remove or disable access [M-0346].
  • Financial benefit: the provider must not receive a financial benefit directly attributable to the infringement where it has the right and ability to control it [M-0347].
  • Notice: on a compliant notice the provider must respond expeditiously to remove or disable the claimed material [M-0348].
  • Designated agent: the safe harbor applies only if an agent is designated, made available on the service including its website, and provided to the Copyright Office [M-0349].
  • Notice contents: an effective notice needs the signature of a person authorized to act for the owner of an exclusive right [M-0350]. It also needs a statement under penalty of perjury that the sender is authorized to act for the owner [M-0351]. The perjury statement attaches to authority, not to the claim that the use infringes [M-0351].
  • Repeat infringers: the provider must have adopted, reasonably implemented and told subscribers about a policy to terminate repeat infringers in appropriate circumstances. The limitations apply only if it did [M-0357].
  • Monitoring: the safe harbor is not conditioned on monitoring the service or seeking out infringement, except as consistent with a standard technical measure [M-0358]. This does not switch off red flag knowledge (analysis, from the row note).
  • Counter notice: a counter notification needs a statement under penalty of perjury that the user believes the material was removed by mistake or misidentification [M-0356]. The provider restores the material not less than 10 nor more than 14 business days after receipt, unless the notifier first tells the agent it has gone to court [M-0355].
  • Misrepresentation: a person who knowingly materially misrepresents that material is infringing is liable for damages, costs and attorneys' fees [M-0359].

Agent designation (regulation, 37 CFR 201.38)

  • The designation gives the provider's full legal name and physical street address [M-0352]. Parent and subsidiary are separate entities and need separate designations [M-0353].
  • A designation expires three years after registration unless renewed by amending or resubmitting it [M-0354]. The source is a saved eCFR copy dated 2026-10-26; check the live eCFR before relying on it [M-0352].

Court decisions on knowledge and control

  • Viacom v. YouTube (2d Cir. 2012): the knowledge conditions mean knowledge or awareness of specific infringing activity [M-0362]. Right and ability to control needs something more than the ability to remove or block access [M-0363]. Willful blindness can apply in the 512(c) knowledge analysis [M-0364].
  • Capitol v. Vimeo (2d Cir. 2016): an employee viewing a video with a recognizable copyrighted song does not alone prove red flag knowledge [M-0365]. The test is an ordinary person without specialized music or copyright knowledge [M-0366].
  • UMG v. Shelter, the Veoh case (9th Cir. 2013): the label skipped the notice protocol, which stripped it of the most powerful evidence of knowledge [M-0367].
  • Ventura v. Motherless (9th Cir. 2018), a UGC site: summary judgment for the site on 512(c) was affirmed [M-0370]. Apparent knowledge and red flag are used interchangeably, and infringement must be obvious to a reasonable person [M-0371]. The site's repeat infringer handling was informal, and the court still found the safe harbor. Do not copy that as a model [M-0372]. No holding on music (row note).
  • Mavrix v. LiveJournal (9th Cir. 2017): common law agency decides whether moderators' acts count as the site's own, which can undercut "storage at the direction of a user" [M-0368]. Fact disputes about volunteer moderators as agents led to reversal of summary judgment for the site [M-0369].
