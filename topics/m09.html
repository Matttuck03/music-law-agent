M09 Secondary liability after Cox

Part 1 of 2: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: high.

Answers questions like: Is a mixing platform liable if a user uploads a mix with songs they do not own; Does knowing users infringe make a platform contributorily liable after Cox; What is vicarious liability and does Cox change it; Does a platform still need a repeat infringer policy; Does the DMCA safe harbour still matter; Which pre-2026 cases are overtaken

Parts: 1 2

Bottom line

US federal law, as of 2026-11-10: after Cox v. Sony (U.S. 2026), a service provider is contributorily liable for a user's infringement only if it intended the service be used to infringe, shown by inducement or by a service tailored to infringement. Knowing that some users infringe is not enough [M-0001, M-0221, M-0222, M-0223]. This helps platforms, but it does not touch direct liability, vicarious liability or the section 512 safe harbour, and it gives no licence for music in uploads [M-0252].

Rules

The Supreme Court decision (US federal, court decision)

  • The Court decided Cox Communications v. Sony Music (No. 24-171) on March 25, 2026, reversing and remanding [M-0001, M-0226].
  • Held: a provider is "contributorily liable for a user's infringement only if it intended that the provided service be used for infringement" [M-0221].
  • Mere knowledge that a service will be used to infringe is insufficient to show that intent [M-0222].
  • Intent can be shown two ways: inducement, or a service tailored to infringement (not capable of substantial or commercially significant noninfringing uses) [M-0223].
  • The Fourth Circuit's knowledge plus insufficient action rule went beyond those two bases and conflicted with the Court's precedent [M-0224].
  • Justice Sotomayor, concurring in the judgment, said other common law theories such as aiding and abetting could apply in copyright. That is a concurrence, not the holding [M-0225].
  • Cox is an internet access provider case, but the holding is stated for any "provider of a service" [M-0252]. Applying it to an app is (analysis) a fair reading, not a ruling on apps.
  • Only contributory liability was before the Court. Vicarious liability is a separate theory the Court did not decide [M-0252]. Direct infringement is not addressed in the syllabus [M-0252]. The section 512 safe harbour was not decided [M-0252].

Baseline liability (US federal, statute)

  • Anyone who violates an exclusive right of the copyright owner is an infringer [M-0220]. A platform that itself copies, performs or distributes music is a direct infringer, whatever Cox says (analysis).

Overtaken or vacated lower decisions (court decisions)

  • Fourth Circuit (2024) affirmed willful contributory liability and reversed vicarious liability for lack of profit from subscribers' infringement [M-0228]. The contributory holding was reversed by the Supreme Court [M-0226]. Cite as history only.
  • Fifth Circuit (2024, Grande) affirmed contributory liability but vacated the damages award [M-0229]. The Supreme Court granted Grande's petition on April 6, 2026, vacated and remanded in light of Cox [M-0002, M-0227]. The remand outcome is not in our sources. Needs source.
  • BMG v. Cox (4th Cir. 2018) held the "knew or should have known" contributory standard is negligence and too low [M-0232]. That standard is overtaken because Cox 2026 requires more than knowledge [M-0224].

The DMCA safe harbour still stands apart (court decisions)

  • BMG v. Cox (4th Cir. 2018): to use the section 512 safe harbour a provider must have adopted and reasonably implemented a policy to terminate repeat infringers [M-0230]. The 2018 holding on 512(i) is not disturbed by Cox 2026 [M-0230].
  • BMG held the safe harbour was lost because the repeat infringer policy was not applied consistently or meaningfully [M-0231, M-0253]. The provider bears the burden of proof on the defence [M-0233].
  • Copyright Office 2020 report (regulator report, not law) notes the Fourth Circuit reads "repeat infringer" as repeat alleged infringer [M-0376]. Cox 2026 did not address that 512(i) reading [M-0376].
  • Viacom v. YouTube (2d Cir. 2012): 512(c) needs knowledge or awareness of specific infringing activity [M-0362]. "Right and ability to control" needs something more than the ability to remove or block [M-0363]. Willful blindness may apply in the 512(c) knowledge context [M-0364].
  • Capitol v. Vimeo (2d Cir. 2016): an employee viewing a video with a recognizable song does not alone create red flag knowledge [M-0365]. The red flag test uses an ordinary person without specialized music or copyright knowledge [M-0366].
  • Ventura v. Motherless (9th Cir. 2018, UGC site): summary judgment for the site on 512(c) was affirmed [M-0370]. Apparent knowledge and red flag mean infringement must be obvious to a reasonable person [M-0371]. The site's informal repeat infringer handling still passed on those facts, so do not treat it as a model [M-0372].
  • Mavrix v. LiveJournal (9th Cir. 2017): common law agency decides whether moderators' acts are the site's own, which can undercut "storage at the direction of a user"; fact disputes over volunteer moderators led to reversal of summary judgment for the site [M-0368, M-0369].
