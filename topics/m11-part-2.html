M11 Sampling and interpolation

Part 2 of 3: Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: medium.

Parts: 1 2 3

Rules

  • Two copyrights exist in a recording: the sound recording (master) and the musical work (composition). Both carry the reproduction right [M-0108].
  • Recording owners hold only the 106(1), (2), (3) and (6) rights, and the recording's reproduction right covers only duplication that recaptures the actual sounds [M-0296, M-0110].
  • Sound-alikes: an independent fixation of other sounds that imitates the recording does not infringe the sound recording copyright, per 114(b) [M-0111]. Narrow: it must be wholly new sounds, not recaptured ones.
  • The musical work remains separate, so (analysis) a sound-alike or replayed interpolation still needs a musical work licence [M-0111]. Right of publicity and false endorsement are not covered here (see M17).
  • Sixth Circuit (court decision): for sampling a sound recording the rule is "Get a license or do not sample", with no de minimis inquiry [M-0186].
  • Ninth Circuit (court decision, VMG Salsoul, 2016): the de minimis exception applies to sound recording sampling, and the court disagrees with the Sixth Circuit [M-0188, M-0189, M-0021].
  • The Ninth Circuit reads the statute and history as keeping de minimis for recordings [M-0190].
  • Ninth Circuit test: a taking is de minimis only if the average audience would not recognize the appropriation [M-0191]. A three-note sample of a composition (licensed recording) was de minimis in Newton v. Diamond [M-0192].
  • Other circuits are not settled in the saved set. Venue matters and a plaintiff may choose a forum (analysis, inference) [M-0249].
  • Grand Upright (S.D.N.Y. 1991, district court): preliminary injunction against unlicensed sampling, with a referral to the US Attorney for possible prosecution under 17 U.S.C. 506(a) and 18 U.S.C. 2319.
  • A licence for one copyright does not clear the other. In Estate of Smith v. Cash Money (S.D.N.Y. 2017) the defendants had a licence for the recording but not the composition, and that gap created the composition claim [M-0193, M-0250].
  • Same case: a 35-second sample was held a fair use on its facts (district court) [M-0212]. Fair use is an affirmative defence, and the party asserting it bears the burden [M-0211]. This case is an adverse example for "licence one, skip the other" only in that fair use happened to win; it is not a safe harbour [M-0250].
  • Fair use: 17 U.S.C. 107 sets a four factor, non-exhaustive test and states no number of seconds [M-0203]. The fourth factor is market effect [M-0204]. Background music in a monetised video must be argued on its facts (analysis) [M-0251].
  • Proving a sample or copy claim for compositions: the Ninth Circuit en banc rejected the inverse ratio rule [M-0194]. In the Fifth Circuit even overwhelming proof of access does not establish factual copying without probative similarity [M-0195], and copying can be inferred without access only if works are strikingly similar [M-0196]. Batiste affirmed summary judgment for defendants on no evidence of copying [M-0197].
  • Interpolation claims turn on protectable expression. Commonplace elements without an original combination are not protected (Ninth Circuit, Gray v. Hudson) [M-0200]. Sheeran (2d Cir. 2024): a selection and arrangement claim built on a four chord progression and syncopated harmonic rhythm failed as a matter of law [M-0202]. For pre-1978 works the 1909 Act protects the composition as defined by the deposited sheet music [M-0201].
  • Williams v. Gaye (9th Cir. 2018): the Blurred Lines verdict stood because there was not an absolute absence of evidence of similarity [M-0198, M-0199].
  • Statutory licences do not reach a short video: a "digital audio transmission" excludes any audiovisual work [M-0112, M-0297], and the 114(d)(2) licence also requires a non-interactive service [M-0115, M-0298]. 114 would in any case not clear the composition [M-0313].
