M11 Sampling and interpolation

Part 1 of 3: Bottom line. Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: medium.

Answers questions like: Can a user put a short sample of a song or beat in a mix; Is a tiny or unrecognizable sample safe (de minimis); Does re-recording a riff or a sound-alike avoid the recording owner; If a platform licenses the recording does it also need the composition; Does fair use cover a few seconds of a sample; Does splitting or re-encoding user audio create sampling risk

Parts: 1 2 3

Bottom line

  • US federal law: an unlicensed sample needs a licence from the recording owner (master) and, if the composition is recognizably used, from the composition owner (publisher). Which of two circuits you are in decides whether "too small to matter" works as a defence [M-0249, M-0021]. The two-licence point rests on [M-0108, M-0193].
  • Re-recording a riff (interpolation) or a sound-alike avoids the master owner only. The composition is still a separate copyright and still needs a licence [M-0111].
  • No statute gives a safe number of seconds, and fair use is argued case by case [M-0203, M-0251].
  • The section 114 statutory licence does not cover samples in video, so direct sync and master licences are needed [M-0112, M-0312]. (analysis) We have no saved row on section 115.

  2. [M-0021] VERIFIED. The Sixth Circuit (Bridgeport, 2005) applies no de minimis defense to sound recording sampling; the Ninth Circuit (VMG Salsoul, 2016) does, creating a circuit split.

    Source: VMG Salsoul, LLC v. Ciccone, 824 F.3d 871 (9th Cir. 2016) (de minimis sampling). Pinpoint: 824 F.3d 871, opinion intro, slip op. pp.4-5 (reporter pin 874 likely, saved text unpaginated). Jurisdiction: case-law. Checked 2026-11-10.

    Quote: "We recognize that the Sixth Circuit held to the contrary in Bridgeport Music, Inc. v. Dimension Films, 410 F.3d 792 (6th Cir. 2005), but--like the leading copyright treatise and several district courts--we find Bridgeport's reasoning unpersuasive."

  5. [M-0111] VERIFIED. Sound-alike recordings that are an independent fixation do not infringe the sound recording copyright (114(b) imitation rule). The musical work remains a separate copyright, so (analysis) a sound-alike still needs a musical work licence.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 114 scope of exclusive rights in sound recordings. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 114(b). Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-10.

    Quote: "do not extend to the making or duplication of another sound recording that consists entirely of an independent fixation of other sounds, even though such sounds imitate or simulate those in the copyrighted sound recording."
