M16 Voice clones and digital replicas

Part 2 of 3: Rules (continued). Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: high.

Parts: 1 2 3

Rules (continued)

  • (regulator guidance) The Copyright Office report (July 2024) recommended a federal replica law with a notice safe harbor, and liability only on actual knowledge [M-0481, M-0482]. A recommendation, not law.
  • The 2025 text differs from 2026: failure to register an agent was conclusive proof of no good faith in S. 1367, but a rebuttable presumption after 90 days in S. 4591 and H.R. 8915 [M-0499, M-0503, M-0471]. S. 1367 as introduced has the same UGC definition and stay down duty [M-0495, M-0497]. H.R. 2794 matches S. 1367 on definition, takedown and stay down [M-0500].
  • H.R. 8915 has the same takedown and stay down conditions as S. 4591 [M-0501, M-0502].

US federal (law in force)

  • Lanham Act 43(a)(1)(A) bars false or misleading statements likely to cause confusion about affiliation, sponsorship or approval. It is the base for a false endorsement claim when a cloned voice suggests a singer backs a product [M-0462].
  • (court decision) Midler v. Ford (9th Cir. 1988): deliberately imitating a widely known singer's distinctive voice to sell a product is a California tort. Narrow: distinctive, widely known, deliberate, to sell [M-0458].
  • (court decision) Waits v. Frito-Lay (9th Cir. 1992) affirmed voice misappropriation and Lanham Act false endorsement over a soundalike ad, but vacated the Lanham damages award as duplicative [M-0459].
  • (court decision) Lehrman v. Lovo (S.D.N.Y., 2025-07-10), motion to dismiss stage: voice clone claims mostly failed under federal trademark and copyright law but could proceed under N.Y. Civil Rights Law 50 and 51. Copyright claims on use of an original recording, contract and consumer claims survived. Later history unchecked [M-0460, M-0461].

Tennessee

  • (state statute) The ELVIS Act (Pub. Ch. 588, 2024) protects an individual's voice, including a simulation of the voice [M-0019, M-0444]. Effective 2024-07-01 [M-0443].
  • Liability: publishing, performing, distributing or making available a voice or likeness knowing it was not authorized [M-0443].
  • It also reaches distributors of a tool whose primary purpose is producing a particular person's voice or likeness, with knowledge it was unauthorized [M-0445].

California

  • (state statute) Civ. Code 3344: knowing use of another's name, voice, signature, photograph or likeness on goods or to advertise or sell, without prior consent, is actionable [M-0446].
  • Civ. Code 3344.1 defines digital replica as a computer generated, highly realistic representation readily identifiable as a person's voice or visual likeness. It excludes authorized sampling, remixing and mastering [M-0447].
  • For a deceased personality, producing or making available a replica in an expressive audiovisual work or sound recording without consent carries the greater of $10,000 or actual damages, subject to listed exceptions [M-0448].

New York

  • (state statute) Civil Rights Law 50 makes use of a living person's name, portrait, picture, likeness or voice for advertising or trade without written consent a misdemeanor. Section 51 gives an injunction and damages for knowing use [M-0450, M-0451].
  • Section 50-f: using a deceased performer's replica in an audiovisual work, sound recording or live musical performance, knowing it was unauthorized, creates liability. The right ends 40 years after death [M-0452, M-0453].
  • Section 50-f(9) exempts owners and employees of an advertising medium, including streaming services, unless they had actual knowledge by prior notice. Scope is advertising media and does not obviously cover a UGC host (analysis) [M-0454].
  • General Obligations Law 5-302(1) voids a services contract clause that allows a replica in place of in person work, if it lacks a reasonably specific description of uses and meets the other conditions. All conditions are needed [M-0455].
