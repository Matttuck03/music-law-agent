M16 Voice clones and digital replicas

Part 1 of 3: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-10. Volatility: high.

Answers questions like: Can a user put an AI clone of a famous singer's voice in a mix; Is a platform liable for a user mix that contains a voice clone; Does the NO FAKES Act apply to a mixing platform or a processing tool; Which states protect voice; Does a soundalike need a licence; What does the UK protect

Parts: 1 2 3

Bottom line

  • Jurisdiction first: US federal law has no digital replica statute today. The NO FAKES Act (S. 4591, H.R. 8915) is a bill, not law. As of the saved status file (updated 2026-08-10, last listed action 2026-06-24) S. 4591 was reported and on the Senate calendar, with no floor vote in the file [M-0003, M-0004, M-0465]. H.R. 8915 was referred to committee with no later action in a file updated 2026-10-23 [M-0466].
  • Today the rules are state law (Tennessee, California, New York, Illinois), the Lanham Act false endorsement claim, and older voice cases from the Ninth Circuit [M-0443, M-0446, M-0450, M-0456, M-0458, M-0462].
  • A voice clone is its own right, apart from the composition and the master. A licence to a song does not clear a clone of a singer's voice. The person whose voice is copied (or an heir, or a label or union holding the right by contract) grants consent. Nobody here can promise a voice is cleared. (analysis)
  • Whether any state or bill rule applies to a given platform or tool is a question for a licensed attorney. This card is not legal advice.
  • For a platform that hosts user uploads, the main US risks are state voice claims against the person who made the clone and, under some statutes, against those who distribute it knowing it is unauthorized [M-0443, M-0456]. A tool that only re-encodes a user's own file privately likely sits outside the bill's online service definition (unverified) [M-0488].

Rules

US federal (pending bills, not law)

  • S. 1367 and H.R. 2794 (2025) were introduced 2025-04-09 and went no further than committee referral [M-0005, M-0463, M-0464]. S. 4591 and H.R. 8915 (2026) were introduced 2026-05-20 [M-0003].
  • S. 4591 was reported with a substitute on 2026-06-24 and placed on the calendar (Calendar No. 446). Status file updated 2026-08-10, last listed action 2026-06-24, and may have moved [M-0004, M-0465].
  • (pending bill) S. 4591 as reported defines online service to include a site or app that predominantly gives public access to user uploaded material. A UGC short-video platform would appear to fit (analysis) [M-0467, M-0487].
  • (pending bill) Safe harbor conditions for a UGC service: remove or disable access to a noticed replica as soon as feasible [M-0469]; also remove later uploads that match the replica's fingerprint, called stay down [M-0470]; register a designated agent with the Copyright Office [M-0471]. The repeat violator policy condition is written for a different provider prong, not the UGC prong [M-0468, M-0487].
  • (pending bill) Without the safe harbor, a UGC service is liable only if its agent received a compliant notice or court order, or it willfully avoided one [M-0472]. Voluntary use of removal tools does not alone give actual knowledge [M-0477].
  • (pending bill) The extra exclusions (news, documentary, commentary, parody, fleeting use) do not apply to a replica used to depict sexually explicit conduct [M-0473].
  • (pending bill) Section 230 would not shield a platform, because the right is treated as intellectual property [M-0475]. The Copyright Office recommended the same carve out in 2024 [M-0484].
  • (pending bill) Preemption: state claims over replicas in expressive works are preempted, but state laws existing on 2025-01-02 and state laws on sexually explicit or election replicas are saved [M-0474]. The Office's 2024 recommendation was a federal floor with no full preemption [M-0483].
  • (pending bill) The right lasts 10 years after death, renewable in 5 year periods on proof of use, and ends 70 years after death [M-0476].
