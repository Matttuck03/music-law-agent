M21 Remixes and derivative works

Part 2 of 3: Rules (continued), Platform application, Gotchas, Open questions for counsel. Law last checked 2026-11-13. Volatility: medium.

Parts: 1 2 3

Rules (continued)

  • Ownership of an authorised remix, US federal: a work is made for hire if an employee makes it within the job, or if it is specially commissioned, falls in a listed category and the parties sign a writing that says so [M-0537, M-0046]. The list does not name sound recordings (analysis row) [M-0047]. For a work made for hire the employer or commissioning party is the author [M-0048].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 201(d)(2) and 204(a): any exclusive right, or a slice of one, can be transferred and owned separately [M-0539]. A transfer is not valid without a writing signed by the owner of the right [M-0040].
  • US federal (policy paper, Commerce Department white paper, January 2016, not law): remixes include mashups and sampling, and in many cases will be derivative works, compilations or collective works [M-0554]. Because remixes often rely on several copyrighted works, they can raise daunting licensing issues [M-0555].
  • Same paper: the Task Force found no proven need for a remix exception or a compulsory remix licence. It recommended guidelines, more voluntary licensing and education [M-0556]. It said some remixes will clearly fall on one side of the fair use line and others will be uncertain [M-0557].

Platform application

  • A user blends two hit records. That mix can involve four owners: two compositions and two masters (analysis) [M-0025, M-0522]. Mixing more tracks adds more owners. This is the licensing problem the 2016 paper describes [M-0555].
  • Who must authorise: both owners of each track, because the mix rearranges or alters the actual recorded sounds [M-0523] and each owner controls derivative works of its own work [M-0522]. No row in this base lets a platform or a user skip either side.
  • Who owns a user's mix: at most, the user owns the new material the user added [M-0529]. If the tracks were used without permission, the unlawful parts get no protection [M-0528]. In the Second Circuit a user could still claim the original additions if the use is a fair use [M-0535]. The Seventh Circuit took a harder line [M-0532]. Other circuits are not in the saved set.
  • Platform terms should not tell users "your mix is yours" without limits (analysis). The accurate line is: you own what you add, not the songs or recordings you used [M-0529].
  • Official remix projects (an artist or label invites remixes): put the grant in a signed writing [M-0040]. Do not assume a commissioned remix is a work made for hire, since the commissioned categories are a closed list and sound recordings are not named [M-0046, M-0047]. The base has no saved source on standard remix deal terms (gap).
  • Re-played versions: if a user re-records a part with new sounds, the master owner drops out but the composition owner does not [M-0111]. A stem pulled from the original recording is not a re-play, because it holds the actual recorded sounds (analysis) [M-0110].
  • Fair use is a defence the user or platform would have to prove [M-0211]. A mix that plays songs as songs, for listening, shares the purpose of the originals, which Warhol says weighs against fair use when the use is commercial [M-0551]. The base has no case on whether a DJ mix or mashup is a fair use (gap).
  • Risk carriers: the user who makes and shares the remix comes first. The platform carries risk as host, as tool maker and for anything it copies or performs itself (see cards M23 and M25). Claimants are labels and publishers.

Gotchas

  • "Change it enough and it is yours" is wrong. Pickett rejected the idea that a stranger may make a derivative work when the original does not pervade it [M-0534]. Warhol warns that adding new expression is not enough on its own [M-0552].
  • "I own my remix because I made it" is at best half right. Protection covers only what you added [M-0529] and drops out where the borrowed material was used unlawfully [M-0528].
  • Pickett is one circuit and its own wording is limited to that case [M-0532]. Keeling is not a music case and rested on a fair use finding [M-0536]. Do not present either as the national rule.
  • A sound-alike avoids only the master. The composition still needs a licence [M-0111].
  • Giving credit and not charging are not defences in the infringement statute (analysis) [M-0220]. Commercial nature is one fair use factor, not the test [M-0549, M-0553].
  • The white paper is a 2016 policy paper, not law. As of 2026-11-13 the saved set has no later statute or Copyright Office action on a remix exception. Check before relying on [M-0556].
  • Anderson v. Stallone is often quoted on this topic. The base has no primary text for it, so it is not cited here.
  • Joint authorship of a remix (two users, or a user and the original artist) is not covered in the base (gap).

Open questions for counsel

  • US federal: when a platform's tool renders the mix, who is the maker of the derivative work, the user or the platform?
  • US federal: outside the Second and Seventh Circuits, can a user claim copyright in the new parts of an unauthorised mix?
  • US federal: can a transition-only blend, where each track plays mostly unchanged, be a fair use, and does the answer differ for a mashup that layers two tracks?
  • US federal: for artist-approved remix projects, what grant wording gives users a safe right to make and share remixes, and who should own the result?
  • US federal: has anything since January 2016 changed the white paper position that there is no remix exception or compulsory remix licence?
