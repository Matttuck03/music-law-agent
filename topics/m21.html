M21 Remixes and derivative works

Part 1 of 3: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-13. Volatility: medium.

Answers questions like: Who has to give permission for a remix; Who owns a remix; Can I protect a remix I made without permission; Does changing a song enough make it mine; Is a re-played version safer than using the original recording; Can a remix or mashup be fair use

Parts: 1 2 3

Bottom line

  • US federal law. A remix of a released track touches two copyrights: the song (composition) and the recording (master). They are separate works [M-0025]. Each owner holds its own right to make derivative works and to let others make them [M-0522]. So a remix built from the original audio needs a yes from both sides (analysis).
  • Who usually says yes: the publisher or songwriter for the composition, the label or distributor for the master (industry practice, see card M01).
  • The statute names remixing. The master owner's derivative right covers works in which the actual recorded sounds are "rearranged, remixed, or otherwise altered in sequence or quality" [M-0523].
  • A remix made without permission gets no copyright protection in any part that uses the borrowed material unlawfully [M-0528]. Changing the song a lot does not fix that [M-0534, M-0552].
  • A remix made with permission: the remixer can own only the new material added, never the underlying song or recording [M-0529].

Rules

  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 101: a derivative work is a work based on one or more earlier works. The examples include a musical arrangement and a sound recording, and "any other form in which a work may be recast, transformed, or adapted" [M-0521, M-0035].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 106(2): the copyright owner has the exclusive right to prepare derivative works and to authorise others to do so [M-0522, M-0565].
  • US federal (regulator guidance, Copyright Office Circular 56A): a sound recording is a separate work from the composition under it [M-0025]. The Office treats the recording as a derivative work of the composition [M-0026].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 114(b): the master owner's derivative right is limited to works where the actual sounds fixed in the recording are rearranged, remixed or otherwise altered in sequence or quality [M-0523]. The 1976 House Report says the wording was added to make that scope clear [M-0524].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 114(b): the master owner's reproduction right covers only copying that recaptures the actual sounds [M-0110]. A new recording made wholly of independently recorded sounds does not infringe the master, even if it imitates it. The composition is still a separate copyright (analysis in the row) [M-0111].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 103(a): protection for a work that uses earlier copyrighted material does not extend to any part where that material was used unlawfully [M-0528]. The Copyright Office says the same and adds that unauthorised adaptation may be infringement (regulator guidance) [M-0527].
  • US federal (legislative history): the House Report says an unauthorised translation of a novel could not be copyrighted at all, while a use that is not unlawful, for example a fair use, leaves the new work copyrightable [M-0530].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 103(b): copyright in a derivative work or compilation covers only the new material the author added. It gives no right in the earlier material [M-0529]. Copyright Office Circular 14 says the same (regulator guidance) [M-0531].
  • Seventh Circuit (court decision, Pickett v. Prince, 2000): section 103(a) does not let a stranger make a derivative work. The court read it, at least so far as that case went, to mean only that the right to make one does not authorise using material that infringes someone else's copyright [M-0532].
  • Seventh Circuit (court decision, Pickett): the court found it very difficult to see how a derivative work not made by the owner of the original could fail to infringe it [M-0533]. It rejected the idea that a stranger may make one as long as the original does not pervade the new work [M-0534].
  • Second Circuit (court decision, Keeling v. Hars, 2015): the maker of an unauthorised work that stays within fair use and adds enough originality may claim protection for her own original contributions [M-0535]. The facts were a parody stage show, not music, and a jury had found fair use [M-0536].
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 501(a): anyone who violates an exclusive right of the copyright owner is an infringer [M-0220]. That sentence has no carve-out for giving credit or for not charging money (analysis).
  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 107: fair use is a four factor test. The factors are purpose and character, nature of the work, amount used, and market effect [M-0547]. The statute sets no number of seconds [M-0203]. It is judged case by case with no bright-line rules (court decision, Campbell) [M-0205].
  • US federal (court decision, Campbell, 1994): parody needs to mimic the original to make its point, so it has some claim to use it. Satire can stand on its own and needs a reason for the borrowing [M-0548]. Commercial use is only one element of factor one [M-0549]. The amount taken is judged against the purpose of the copying [M-0550].
  • US federal (court decision, Warhol, 2023): if the original and the new use share the same or a very similar purpose and the use is commercial, factor one is likely to weigh against fair use unless there is another justification [M-0551]. Treating any added new expression as enough would let "transformative use" swallow the owner's derivative right [M-0552]. Commercial nature is relevant but does not decide the matter [M-0553].
  • Southern District of New York (court decision, Estate of Smith, 2017): fair use is a defence, and the party who raises it carries the burden [M-0211].
