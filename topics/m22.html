M22 Covers and the compulsory mechanical licence

Part 1 of 2: Bottom line, Rules, Platform application. Law last checked 2026-11-13. Volatility: medium.

Answers questions like: What does a cover licence allow; Can I change the arrangement of a song I cover; Does a cover licence cover a remix or mashup; Why does a cover in a video need a sync licence; Does a cover licence let me use the original recording; What do distributors require for a cover

Parts: 1 2

Bottom line

  • US federal law. A cover is your own new recording of someone else's song. Section 115 gives a compulsory "mechanical" licence to make and distribute audio recordings of a song that has already been released, without asking the publisher, if you follow the statute's steps [M-0339, M-0545].
  • The licence is for the composition only. It is not a licence of any sound recording [M-0542]. You may not copy the original recording with it [M-0543].
  • You may arrange the song to fit your style. You may not change the basic melody or the fundamental character of the work [M-0540].
  • It is audio only. It does not cover putting the song in a video. Video needs a sync licence agreed directly with the publisher [M-0544, M-0121, M-0140].
  • A remix or mashup built from the original audio is not a cover, and a cover licence does not reach it (analysis from [M-0542, M-0540]).

Rules

  • US federal, 17 U.S.C. 115: the rights to make and to distribute phonorecords (audio copies) of nondramatic musical works are subject to compulsory licensing [M-0339]. The licence is limited to phonorecords made mainly for distribution to the public for private use [M-0120].
  • First release belongs to the owner. US federal, 115(a)(1)(A): the compulsory licence is open only if phonorecords of the song were already distributed to the public in the US with the owner's authority. For a digital music provider's digital deliveries there is a second path: the first recording was made with the composition owner's authority and the recording owner authorised the deliveries [M-0545].
  • Arrangement limit. US federal, 115(a)(2): the licence includes the privilege of making an arrangement only as far as needed to fit the style or manner of the performance. The arrangement may not change the basic melody or fundamental character of the work [M-0540].
  • No new copyright in the arrangement. US federal, 115(a)(2): an arrangement made under the compulsory licence is not protected as a derivative work unless the composition owner gives express consent [M-0541].
  • Composition only. US federal, 115(a)(1): the licence is for making and distributing phonorecords of the musical work. It is not a licence of any sound recording [M-0542]. The Copyright Office says you cannot copy a publicly distributed sound recording without permission, cannot change the basic melody, and cannot claim your arrangement as a derivative work (regulator guidance, Circular 73) [M-0543].
  • No video. The Copyright Office says a phonorecord does not include sounds that accompany a motion picture or other audiovisual work, so the compulsory licence does not cover a song in video (regulator guidance) [M-0544]. The 1976 House Report says the same about sound tracks [M-0121]. The base's own reading reaches the same result for the blanket licence (analysis row) [M-0179].
  • Sync is a free-market licence. The sync right is the industry term for the reproduction right in a song timed to pictures [M-0141]. The Copyright Office recommended in 2015 that consumer audiovisual uses stay in the free market with no compulsory licence (regulator report) [M-0140].
  • Notice for physical copies. US federal, 115(b): for non-digital phonorecords, a notice of intention must be served before, or within 30 days after, making the phonorecords and before distributing them. Missing it closes off the compulsory licence and, without a voluntary licence, makes the making and distribution infringement [M-0546].
  • Digital services. US federal, 115(d): a digital music provider can get a blanket mechanical licence through the Mechanical Licensing Collective for digital phonorecord deliveries [M-0023, M-0122]. The blanket licence covers the musical works available for compulsory licensing for covered activities [M-0125]. The base has no row on how notices changed over in 2021 (gap). Rates are in card M04 and are not quoted here.
  • A cover avoids the master owner only if every sound is newly recorded. US federal, 114(b): an independent recording of other sounds does not infringe the sound recording copyright, even if it imitates the original [M-0111].
  • Playing a cover to the public is a different right. Public performance of the song is separate from the reproduction and distribution rights that the mechanical licence covers (analysis row) [M-0183]. See card M05 for performing rights organisations.
  • CD Baby (platform terms, help page, web archive copy dated 2026-02-16, captured 2026-11-13): a cover release needs a mechanical licence for digital downloads, which covers the composition but not the original recording [M-0596]. Derivative works such as samples cannot be cleared through its cover song process [M-0597]. Even for streaming-only plans it still requires at least a digital download licence to distribute a cover [M-0598].
  • TuneCore (platform terms, help page, web archive copy dated 2020-05-07, captured 2026-11-13, possibly stale): a cover may need permission and possibly a licence from the original copyright holder before release [M-0594]. The same 2020 page says some stores did not accept soundalike covers [M-0595].

Platform application

  • A user records their own performance of a released song, audio only. That is a cover. The composition side can run through the compulsory mechanical licence [M-0339, M-0545]. The master belongs to whoever made the new recording (analysis) [M-0111].
  • A user mixes the original recording of a song. That is not a cover. It uses the actual recorded sounds, which sit inside the master owner's rights [M-0110, M-0523], and the cover licence is not a licence of any sound recording [M-0542].
  • A user layers the vocal of one song over the instrumental of another. Even on the composition side this is likely past the arrangement privilege, because it goes beyond fitting a style of performance (analysis) [M-0540]. It needs the publishers' direct consent, and the masters' owners too if original audio is used [M-0522].
  • A user changes tempo, key or structure of a cover so far that the melody or character changes. That is outside the compulsory licence and needs the publisher's consent (analysis) [M-0540].
  • A user makes a video of a cover or a mix. The mechanical licence stops at audio [M-0544]. A sync licence from the publisher is needed, and a master use licence too if the original recording is used [M-0140, M-0141].
  • If the platform streams covers on demand, it also needs public performance licences for the compositions (see card M05) [M-0183]. Whether the platform can itself use the blanket mechanical licence depends on whether it qualifies as a digital music provider. That is a question for counsel [M-0122].
  • If users send covers to stores through a distributor, the distributor's own rules apply on top of the law. The two saved pages both treat covers and sample-based works differently [M-0597, M-0594].
  • Risk carriers: the user who records and releases the cover; the platform if it hosts, streams or distributes it; publishers as claimants. Labels become claimants only when original audio is used.
