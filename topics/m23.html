M23 Stem tools and DJ mixes

Part 1 of 4: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-13. Volatility: high.

Answers questions like: Is it legal to host or stream DJ mixes of commercial songs; Is the maker of a stem splitter or mixing tool liable for what users do; Does the statutory webcasting licence cover saved or on-demand mixes; How do licensed mix services clear the tracks; What does a live DJ stream need; Is splitting a song into stems a copy

Parts: 1 2 3 4

Bottom line

  • US federal law. Nothing in this base lets a platform publish DJ mixes of commercial tracks without licences. Every track in a mix carries two copyrights, the song and the recording [M-0025]. A recorded mix copies both [M-0108]. Playing it to the public performs the songs [M-0106] and, online, the recordings [M-0107].
  • The section 114 statutory licence does not fit a library of saved mixes that people play on demand. It bars interactive services [M-0115], archived programs under 5 hours [M-0578] and longer archives kept up more than 2 weeks [M-0579].
  • The licensed mix services in the saved set describe direct deals with labels and paying rights holders in each mix. Neither says it relies on the statutory licence (company statements, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0589, M-0593].
  • A tool maker is not contributorily liable just because the tool can be misused [M-0560, M-0563]. It is exposed when it promotes or shapes the tool for infringement [M-0562, M-0221]. No saved case applies this to a stem tool, and vicarious liability is a separate question [M-0252].
  • Four things are unsettled or missing here: whether splitting stems is itself an infringing copy, whether a mix is a compilation or a derivative work, what a club or venue licence covers, and live-stream terms of services other than Mixcloud. This card says so and does not fill them.

Rules

  • Tool makers, the old baseline. US federal (court decision, Sony v. Universal, 1984): selling copying equipment is not contributory infringement if the product is widely used for legitimate purposes or is merely capable of substantial noninfringing uses [M-0560]. The Court found the video recorder had such uses, in part because private, noncommercial home time-shifting of broadcast television was fair use [M-0561]. That holding is about TV recorders. No saved case applies it to a stem tool (analysis).
  • Tool makers, inducement. US federal (court decision, Grokster, 2005): one who distributes a device with the object of promoting its use to infringe, shown by clear expression or other affirmative steps, is liable for the resulting infringement by others [M-0562]. Mere knowledge that a product could be or is used to infringe is not enough, and ordinary product support is not inducement [M-0563].
  • Services, the current rule. US federal (court decision, Cox v. Sony, decided March 25, 2026): contributory liability needs intent that the service be used for infringement, shown only by inducement or by a service tailored to infringement [M-0001, M-0221, M-0223]. Mere knowledge that the service will be used to infringe is not enough [M-0222]. The Court rejected the lower court's knowledge plus inaction rule [M-0224].
  • What Cox left open. Cox was about contributory liability. Vicarious liability is a separate theory the Court did not decide (analysis row) [M-0252]. One Justice said other common-law theories such as aiding and abetting could apply [M-0225].
  • Applying inducement to a stem or mixing tool: the researchers' reading is that a tool maker whose marketing or onboarding steers users to split and mix commercial songs they do not own is exposed through its own statements and design, not through the tool's ability alone (unverified) [M-0564].
  • Direct liability. US federal, 17 U.S.C. 501(a): anyone who violates an exclusive right is an infringer [M-0220]. When the platform's own servers copy, split, render or stream music, the question becomes whether the platform itself did the act. The base has no row on that test (gap).
  • Hosting user uploads. US federal, 17 U.S.C. 512(c): the safe harbor covers infringement by reason of storage at the direction of a user [M-0343]. Conditions include no actual or red flag knowledge [M-0344, M-0345], fast removal on knowledge or notice [M-0346, M-0348], no direct financial benefit from infringement the provider can control [M-0347], a designated agent [M-0349] and a repeat infringer policy [M-0357]. Card M08 has the detail.
