M25 DJ-mix platform application, feature by feature

Part 1 of 5: Bottom line, Rules. Law last checked 2026-11-13. Volatility: high.

Answers questions like: Which features of a mixing app need licences; Is private mixing on my own device lower risk than sharing; What happens when users upload commercial tracks; Can users export or download their mixes; Does charging money change the legal risk; Do Canada, Australia, the UK or the EU treat user remixes differently

Parts: 1 2 3 4 5

Bottom line

  • Risk rises in steps: mixing on the user's own device, then saving, then storing on the platform's servers, then sharing with the public, then downloads and paid features. Each step adds rights that need a licence and moves more of the risk from the user to the platform (analysis from the rows below).
  • Nothing in the base makes any step fully safe with unlicensed commercial tracks. Private, on-device mixing is the lowest step, but whether it is lawful is unsettled, not cleared [M-0110, M-0575].
  • Public sharing of mixes of commercial tracks needs licences for both the songs and the recordings [M-0301, M-0106, M-0107]. The statutory webcasting licence does not cover saved or on-demand mixes [M-0115, M-0578].
  • Country rules differ sharply. Canada has a non-commercial user content exception for individuals [M-0616]. The UK and EU have narrow parody and pastiche exceptions [M-0240, M-0631]. Australia's saved sections cover parody or satire only [M-0622]. None of them is a general licence for a mix platform.

Rules

  • US federal: each track is two works, a song and a recording [M-0025]. Both carry a reproduction right [M-0108]. Each owner controls derivative works [M-0522]. For recordings that reaches works where the actual sounds are rearranged, remixed or altered [M-0523].
  • US federal: songs carry a public performance right [M-0106]. Recordings carry one only for digital audio transmissions [M-0107]. Anyone who violates an exclusive right is an infringer [M-0220].
  • US federal, tool maker tests: a product capable of substantial lawful uses is not contributory infringement by itself (court decision, Sony) [M-0560]. Promoting infringing use by clear words or affirmative steps creates liability (court decision, Grokster) [M-0562]. Contributory liability needs intent, shown by inducement or a service tailored to infringement (court decision, Cox v. Sony, 2026) [M-0221, M-0223].
  • US federal, hosting: the 512(c) safe harbor covers storage at the direction of a user [M-0343], on conditions: no actual or red flag knowledge [M-0344, M-0345], fast removal [M-0346, M-0348], no direct financial benefit from infringement the provider can control [M-0347], a designated agent [M-0349], and a repeat infringer policy [M-0357]. The safe harbor is not conditioned on the provider monitoring its service [M-0358].
  • US federal, 114 statutory licence: not for interactive services [M-0115], not for archived programs under 5 hours [M-0578], not for longer archives kept more than 2 weeks [M-0579], and not if the service induces listeners to make copies [M-0582].
  • US federal, damages: statutory damages run from $750 to $30,000 per work [M-0215], up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement [M-0217], counted per work and not per view (analysis row) [M-0254]. They depend on timely registration [M-0214].
