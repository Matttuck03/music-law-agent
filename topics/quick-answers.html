The myths

1. If I change a song enough, is it mine?

  • No. Only the owner may make or authorise works based on the original [M-0522]. One appeals court rejected the idea that a stranger may make one when the original does not pervade the new work [M-0534]. The Supreme Court warned that adding new expression is not enough by itself [M-0552].
  • You can own only what you added [M-0529], and not even that in parts where others' material is used unlawfully [M-0528].

2. Is anything under a set number of seconds safe?

  • No. The fair use statute has four factors and no number of seconds [M-0203, M-0547]. Courts decide case by case with no bright-line rules [M-0205]. The base's own summary: there is no safe length (analysis row) [M-0251].
  • One trial court found a 35-second sample a fair use of the song (the composition) on its facts. That is one case, not a rule, and the base has not checked its later history [M-0212]. One appeals court said of recordings: get a licence or do not sample [M-0186]. Another allows very small uses only if an average listener would not recognise them [M-0188, M-0191].

3. Does crediting the artist make it fine?

  • No. Anyone who violates an exclusive right is an infringer [M-0220]. That rule has no credit exception (analysis).
  • Credit matters only as one condition inside some foreign exceptions, such as Canada's user content exception [M-0617]. It never works alone.

4. I am not selling it. Is that enough?

  • No. Infringement does not depend on a sale [M-0220] (analysis). Commercial use is only one element of one fair use factor [M-0549, M-0553].
  • Statutory damages are set per work, not by what you earned [M-0215, M-0254].

5. Does a cover licence cover a remix?

  • No. The compulsory cover licence is for the song only and is not a licence of any recording [M-0542]. It bars changes to the basic melody or fundamental character [M-0540].
  • One distributor says derivative works such as samples cannot go through its cover process (web archive copy dated 2026-02-16, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0597].

6. DJs do it in clubs, so is it legal online?

  • That does not follow. Permission to perform a song in public does not supply the right to copy it (analysis row) [M-0183].
  • The base has no saved club or venue licence text, so it makes no claim about what such a licence covers (gap).
  • Online, a licensed mix service says both the song and the recording of every track must be cleared by the service (company blog, captured 2026-11-13) [M-0586].

  1. [M-0522] VERIFIED. The copyright owner has the exclusive right to prepare derivative works, and to authorize others to do so.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 106 exclusive rights in copyrighted works. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 106(2). Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "the owner of copyright under this title has the exclusive rights to do and to authorize any of the following"

  2. [M-0534] VERIFIED. Pickett rejected the idea that a stranger may make a derivative work without permission if the original does not pervade the derivative; it would undermine the 106(2) right.

    Source: Pickett v. Prince, 207 F.3d 402 (7th Cir. 2000). Pinpoint: 207 F.3d 402. Jurisdiction: case-law. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "would inject enormous uncertainty into the law of copyright and undermine the exclusive right that section 106(2) gives the owner of the copyright on the original work"

  3. [M-0552] VERIFIED. Warhol: reading Campbell to favour any use adding new expression would let transformative use swallow the owner's right to prepare derivative works.

    Source: Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508 (2023). Pinpoint: 598 U.S. 508, syllabus. Jurisdiction: case-law. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "transformative use would swallow the copyright owner’s exclusive right to prepare derivative works"

  4. [M-0529] VERIFIED. Copyright in a derivative work or compilation covers only the new material the author contributed and gives no exclusive right in the preexisting material.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 103 compilations and derivative works. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 103(b). Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "The copyright in a compilation or derivative work extends only to the material contributed by the author of such work"

  5. [M-0528] VERIFIED. Under Section 103(a), protection for a work employing preexisting copyrighted material does not extend to any part of the work in which the material has been used unlawfully.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 103 compilations and derivative works. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 103(a). Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "protection for a work employing preexisting material in which copyright subsists does not extend to any part of the work in which such material has been used unlawfully"

  6. [M-0203] VERIFIED. Section 107 makes fair use a four-factor, non-exhaustive test; the statute states no number of seconds.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 107 fair use. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 107. Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-10.

    Quote: "In determining whether the use made of a work in any particular case is a fair use the factors to be considered shall include--"

  7. [M-0547] VERIFIED. Section 107 lists four fair use factors: purpose and character (commercial or nonprofit), nature of the work, amount and substantiality used, and market effect.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 107 fair use. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 107. Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-13.

    Quote: "the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole"

  8. [M-0205] VERIFIED. Campbell: fair use requires case-by-case analysis and is not simplified by bright-line rules.

    Source: Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994) (U.S. Reports via Library of Congress). Pinpoint: 510 U.S. 569, 577 (Part II.A, opinion of the Court). Jurisdiction: case-law. Checked 2026-11-10.

    Quote: "The task is not to be simplified with bright-line rules, for the statute, like the doctrine it recognizes, calls for case-by-case analysis."

  9. [M-0251] VERIFIED. Analysis: fair use is decided case by case on the four factors in 17 U.S.C. 107, including commercial nature and amount used; the statute sets no safe length or seconds rule, so background music in a monetised video must be argued on its facts.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 107 fair use. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 107. Jurisdiction: analysis. Checked 2026-11-10.

  18. [M-0215] VERIFIED. Statutory damages range from $750 to $30,000 per work, as the court considers just, at the owner's election.

    Source: 17 U.S.C. 504 remedies, damages and profits. Pinpoint: 17 U.S.C. 504(c)(1). Jurisdiction: US-federal. Checked 2026-11-10.

  19. [M-0254] VERIFIED. Analysis: statutory damages are awarded per work infringed, up to $150,000 per work if willful (ordinary range $750 to $30,000), and only for works registered in time (s.412); exposure is per work, not per view.
